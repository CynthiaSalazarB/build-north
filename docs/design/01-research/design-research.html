What Reality Check should look like

Research on the style that fits the app, three directions with a reason for every color, and the real apps and sites they come from. I recommend Ink & Highlighter. Mockups come next, once we pick a direction.

Cynthia's rule: every color needs a reason. Each swatch below says what it means in the app.

What the style has to carry

The brief, from our own decisions

A thinking tool, not a study tool

The subject is the gym equipment; thinking is the muscle. It should feel like working something out, not cramming.

The map is the hero

The learner builds a mentefacto: a diagram with four parts. That drawing is the most characteristic thing in the app.

Honest about confidence

The calibration gap (felt sure vs. actually scored) has to read in one glance, without making people feel stupid.

No streaks, no XP

Gamification is progression only: levels, a mastery map, and "you did this alone" moments. So no Duolingo-style reward candy.

Three directions

Pick one, then we mock it up

Each card shows the same end-of-session result, drawn in that direction's colors: predicted 7 of 8, scored 5, so the gap is +2 (overconfident).

Recommended

A · Ink & Highlighter

A student's notebook: graph paper, blue ink, a yellow highlighter. The mentefacto is drawn on paper in class, so the app looks like the place where the thinking already happens.

  • Graph paper #F8F9F6The ground. A faint grid under the map invites the learner to draw on it. Cool white, not cream.
  • Ink blue #1F2E6EQuestions and the learner's own lines. Blue cued exploratory, "approach" thinking in Mehta & Zhu's 2009 study, which is the mode we want while people ask.
  • Highlighter #FFD84DHints and Help only. You highlight what you're unsure about, so yellow means "help is here, and it costs a point."
  • Clay #D9653BThe overconfidence gap. Warm and serious without being an error red: red cued avoidance in the same study, and we want people to keep trying.
  • Moss #2F8F68"You did this alone." Reserved for unassisted wins, so green stays rare and means something.

Inspiration

What to take from each reference

One move to take from each site, and one to leave. Follow the links to see them: screenshots of other companies’ sites stay out of this public repo.

Brilliant

Take: the hero is a live diagram you can touch, not a photo. Our map can do the same job.

Leave: the "personal tutor" pitch. We're the opposite: the app asks, the learner works it out.

Heptabase

Take: "whiteboards and cards to clarify your thinking". The closest reference for the map-building screen.

Leave: the power-user density. Our map has four parts, not a whole knowledge base.

Are.na

Take: text-first calm, one deep navy action, and a tone that respects curious people.

Leave: the near-total absence of color. A learner needs clear signals for hints and wins.

Fatebook

Take: the prediction is a plain, prominent field, the same job as our "how many of the 8 points?" screen.

Leave: percentages. We decided on points, so the guess and the score share one scale.

Headspace

Take: warmth lowers the stakes. Its rounded character makes a hard thing feel safe.

Leave: the mascot. A character competes with the map for attention.

Duolingo

Take: chunky buttons with a solid bottom edge that feel pressable. Good for one-thumb use.

Leave: streaks, gems and the reward loop. We ruled out extrinsic rewards.

Also worth a look (no screenshot)

  • grug, Apple Design Award 2026 for Delight and Fun. A hand-drawn, scribbled look applied everywhere. Proof that a committed sketch style can win, which supports direction A's handwritten notes.
  • Guitar Wiz, Apple Design Award 2026 for Inclusivity. Our bar for accessibility: never use color as the only signal, which is why the gap is a number and a label, not only a color.

Why these colors

What the research supports, and what it doesn't

Blue for open thinking, no red for "wrong"

Mehta and Zhu (Science, 2009), six studies with over 600 people: blue backgrounds doubled creative output in brainstorming, and red improved detail tasks like proofreading by up to 31%. Their explanation: red puts people in a cautious, avoidant mode, blue in an exploratory one.

A later study replicated only the memory effect. So we use it as a reason, not a promise.

Cool colors for learning, dark for anxiety

A 2024 survey of students at two universities in Lahore found cool colors (white, green, blue) preferred for learning spaces and linked with curiosity. Black and grey were linked with anxiety.

It measures how students felt, not how much they learned.

The strongest reason: one color, one job

Most color-psychology claims online are weak. What reliably helps is consistent coding: when yellow always means "help" and green always means "you did it alone," people learn the signals fast. Direction A is built on that.

Not checked yet: color contrast for every pairing (the later rounds check it against WCAG), and font licenses beyond Google Fonts' open licenses.
